## Safety & professionalism practices (established through real practice)

- **Client always types their own password** during any account connection — never Madden, even if sitting right there. This is a genuine selling point ("I never see your password") and needs to stay literally true.
- For remote clients: use screen-share apps (Zoom/Google Meet) with the **"request remote control"** feature for the password-entry moment specifically — client's own hands still do the typing even on a shared screen.
- Before connecting to any account: explain simply what's being requested and why, let the client actually read the real permissions screen together, mention they can revoke access anytime with one click.
- Test automations with a **separate test folder / fake data** before pointing them at a client's real, sensitive data (learned this from the mom project — built the OneDrive automation logic on a test folder first, only moved to her real inbox once basics were proven).
- If a client's account is tied to an employer/organization (not fully self-employed), check whether their organization is actually okay with a third-party tool before connecting anything — real IT-policy consideration, not just a technicality.
- Use a dedicated business email for outreach/contact — not a fully personal one. Avoid publishing personal phone number or face photo on public-facing materials aimed at strangers (safety, given age).

## How to explain "is this safe" to a skeptical client (script used successfully)

> "Make.com is a real automation company, kind of like Zapier — lots of businesses use it. This is Google/Microsoft's own official login screen, not something I built — Make never sees your password. You're welcome to check it out yourself before continuing, just to see it's legit."

> "\[Tool\] only sees one folder/inbox I tell it to — not your whole account, not other files. It's the same kind of permission you already grant lots of apps. You can disconnect it anytime with one click."

## Discovery-conversation method (for finding what to automate for a new client)

Ask open questions, don't pitch first:

- "What's something in your work that feels repetitive or annoying?"
- "Do you ever lose track of messages or forget to follow up?"
- "Is there anything you wish just happened automatically?" Then match their real answer to something already known how to build — don't invent new categories of automation on the spot for a first client conversation.

## Handling "I don't know the answer" moments with a real client

Established as genuinely fine, not a weakness:

- "That's a great question — let me look into it and get back to you."
- "I haven't built that exact thing yet, but I'm confident I can figure it out — can I take a day to check?"
